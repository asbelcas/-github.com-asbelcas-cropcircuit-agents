## Background
I'm an animal scientist (Michigan State) and former park ranger building a regenerative
farm in Southwest Michigan from scratch — land, infrastructure, and a working
demonstration site, funded so far entirely by my own labor. Along the way I became a
live test case for a question economists are only starting to model: what a one-person
business looks like when AI agents do the back office. I run a small AI crew — research,
monitoring, drafting, automation — that works around the clock while I do the parts that
require a body and a name: walking land, caring for animals, and taking responsibility
for every recommendation my business ships.

## The idea
Small farms and rural businesses that cannot economically justify dedicated
administrative staff have a structural staffing problem: research goes undone, grants
expire before anyone applies, customer inquiries sit unanswered, records scatter across
notebooks, and the owner spends high-value daylight on administrative work. AI agents
may change the minimum efficient size of an organization. I want to measure that claim
where it is hardest to fake — on a real farm, with real animals, weather, money, and
consequences.

The grant funds the minimum physical system needed to run that experiment against
reality: water, movable fencing, and basic sensing. The $6,000 turns Crop Circuit from
a digital operating system attached to a planned farm into a measurable field experiment
with physical consequences — decisions about infrastructure, animals, soil, purchasing,
scheduling, outreach, and documentation will have costs and observable results rather
than remaining software demos.

The larger product is the evidence, not my farm. I will publish the workflows, costs,
failure cases, human override points, and operating templates so another small operator
can see exactly what was automated, what remained human, how much it cost, and where
the system failed. The goal is not to prove that "AI is useful." It is to identify which
pieces of a tiny organization's administrative capacity can actually be reproduced with
agents — and which still require scarce human judgment.

**Hypothesis:** A skilled solo operator using a small AI-agent stack can obtain a
meaningful fraction of the research, monitoring, documentation, and business-development
capacity normally supplied by additional staff, while keeping consequential decisions
under human review. I expect some workflows to fail. Those failures are part of the
output: the useful boundary is not "what can AI do?" but "where does AI stop being
cheaper than competent human attention?"

## Why me
I sit on both sides of this experiment. I have a B.S. in Animal Science from Michigan
State and years of field, livestock, dairy, and land-stewardship experience, but I am
also the exact kind of operator this model is meant for: one person with more real work
than one person can administratively support. I build the AI workflows, then I am the
human who has to act on their output in the physical world. If an agent gives bad
advice, I am the one who finds out — I carry the bucket, move the fence, handle the
animal, spend the money, and live with the result. That makes the farm an unusually
unforgiving evaluation environment.

My advantage is not that AI makes me an expert in everything. It is that I already have
domain judgment, and AI lets me apply that judgment across research, monitoring,
documentation, and operations at a scale I could not afford with employees.

The crew is already running: two paid service tiers were designed and launched from
observed farmer problems and rapid market testing ($125 farm-plan review, $350 systems
audit), a monitoring system surfaces public farmer questions and potential customer
needs quickly while I decide whether and how to respond, and a research pipeline turns
35-paper corpora into farm decisions in hours. Everything — including what breaks — is
documented publicly.

## What the money buys (be concrete)
- $3,000 — minimum physical experimental testbed: livestock water system + movable
  rotational fencing for the first animals, so recommendations about grazing, water,
  and infrastructure meet real consequences.
- $1,000 — baseline sensing: soil probes and basic instrumentation, so the farm's data
  is verification-grade rather than anecdote.
- $1,500 — documentation production costs for the public replication playbook: hosting,
  storage, editing, and print materials for field days (compensating my own documentation
  time is not claimed).
- $500 — contingency and direct costs of demonstration days for other small-farm
  operators.
Total: $6,000. If funded partially: the testbed line comes first — it unlocks the rest.

## In 90 days
- Physical testbed operational or ready for first livestock (water + fencing installed).
- Baseline soil/site measurements recorded.
- 3+ agent-assisted workflows documented end-to-end: actual human/agent division of
  labor, cost and time records.
- AI mistakes and my overrides recorded and published, not hidden.
- Human time before/after measured on at least one workflow.
- Public v1 replication guide released; outside farmer/operator feedback collected on
  which pieces they could — or could not — replicate.
- Next experiment selected based on the evidence.

If the model does not save meaningful human time, or proves too brittle outside digital
tasks, I will publish that too.
