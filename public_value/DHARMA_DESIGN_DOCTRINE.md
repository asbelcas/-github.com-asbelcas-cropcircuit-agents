# Dharma-Informed Design Doctrine

CropCircuit needs more than efficient calculations.

A sufficiently capable farm system can optimize almost anything. That does not mean every optimizable outcome is worth pursuing.

This doctrine gives CropCircuit a human-governed value layer for deciding what its tools and future agents should optimize for.

It is inspired by Hindu philosophical concepts discussed in CropCircuit's design work. It is not presented as a complete or authoritative account of Hinduism, which contains many traditions, schools, texts, and interpretations.

## Core axiom

**Let machines handle measurement, prediction, repetitive labor, and coordination. Let dharma determine what the machines are optimizing for.**

The preferred architecture is:

purpose and right relationship
→ hard constraints
→ viability
→ descriptive effects
→ sufficiency
→ uncertainty
→ reversibility
→ decision

A high monetary payoff should not silently purchase permission to violate a hard welfare, ecological, legal, or human boundary.

## Dharma — right relationship

Ask what role a farm, tool, or intervention should fulfill within its actual place.

Questions:
- Does this fit the land, watershed, animals, people, and community involved?
- Does it help the farm fulfill its purpose without degrading the conditions that make the farm possible?
- Are we solving the right problem, or merely optimizing an available metric?

Dharma defines the objective before optimization begins.

## Ahimsa — minimize avoidable harm

Agriculture cannot literally avoid all disturbance or harm.

CropCircuit therefore uses ahimsa as a design constraint:
- make likely harms visible;
- distinguish necessary tradeoffs from avoidable ones;
- avoid hiding animal, ecological, or human costs outside the financial model;
- do not let high ROI automatically override a declared hard boundary.

## Yajna — reciprocity

A durable farm does not only extract. It returns something to the systems that support it.

Ask:
- What is returned to soil?
- What habitat or ecological function is maintained?
- What resilience is added?
- What knowledge is shared?
- Does labor receive rest, safety, dignity, or regained time?
- Does the intervention strengthen future cycles?

## Aparigraha — enoughness

Many optimization systems assume that more is better.

CropCircuit should also ask: **What is enough?**

Enoughness may mean:
- enough income to keep the farm viable;
- enough reserve capacity for bad years;
- enough production to fulfill commitments;
- enough labor reduction to restore human time;
- enough redundancy to survive failure.

Once the stated sufficiency threshold is met, additional throughput should not automatically outrank habitat, beauty, time, redundancy, or resilience.

## Loka-samgraha — wider-system welfare

A decision can be profitable to one operator while weakening the larger system.

Make room for effects on:
- neighbors and local community;
- shared water and soil;
- pollinators and wildlife;
- food-system resilience;
- future operators;
- local knowledge and mutual aid.

## Karma yoga — disciplined action without metric worship

Measure carefully. Act carefully. Observe the result. Learn. Correct.

Do not distort the larger purpose to rescue a bad metric, and do not let one failed season justify abandoning declared principles.

## Cycles over infinite growth

Farms are cyclical systems.

Growth, harvest, death, decomposition, dormancy, succession, fallow, and renewal are not failures of linear progress.

A healthy operating model can include seasonal contraction, fallow, reserve building, maintenance seasons, deliberate rest, succession, and redesign.

## The non-compensation rule

Some values should be modeled as hard constraints, not weighted preferences.

If a hard constraint fails, a higher projected profit should not make the option win through a weighted total score.

This is why CropCircuit should not produce one universal moral score.

## The uncertainty rule

Unknown is not the same as good.

If an important ecological, welfare, legal, financial, or operational effect is uncertain:
- label it uncertain;
- identify evidence that would resolve it;
- prefer reversible experiments when practical;
- avoid pretending precision exists.

## The reversibility rule

When two viable options are close, prefer the option that:
- is easier to reverse;
- preserves future choices;
- creates fewer irreversible dependencies;
- can be tested at small scale;
- fails gracefully.

## Constitutional shorthand

**Measure everything useful. Optimize nothing blindly. Take enough. Return something. Preserve life and future possibility.**

## Software implications

Future CropCircuit tools and agents should separate:
1. hard constraints;
2. viability;
3. descriptive effects;
4. sufficiency;
5. uncertainty;
6. reversibility.

The system should explain tradeoffs rather than collapse them into one opaque score.

## AI behavior rule

AI may calculate labor savings, cash flow, yield effects, maintenance burden, resource requirements, and scenario sensitivity.

AI should not silently redefine the farm's values because one metric improves.

**Capability chooses means only after humans define acceptable ends.**
